# Gateless Gate 3D

*An interactive edition of the Mumonkan, in your browser.*

## [Enter the Gate](https://killedbyapixel.github.io/GatelessGate/)

Works on desktop and phone. No install, no sign-in, nothing to download.

The Mumonkan, "The Gateless Gate," is a collection of forty-eight Zen koans put together in 1228.
Koans are short, strange stories a student sits with until something opens.
This edition turns that book into a place you can visit instead of pages you turn.

Every koan gets its own low-poly diorama: black ink on paper, with one mark of color on whatever the story turns on.
You read the case, the old master's commentary, and the closing verse.
You can also have it read to you, and every scene has its own quiet weather.

## What's inside

- **All forty-nine cases.** Mumon's forty-eight, plus Amban's traditional addition. Each one has its own scene, the full text, and a spoken reading.
- **Scenes that answer back.** Touch is optional and never a puzzle. A flag ruffles and settles, water rings, a bell sounds.
- **Gentle sound.** Wind, bells, the occasional chime. Nothing is loud.
- **A timer for sitting.** From any case you can start a short meditation, opened and closed by a bell.
- **Any order you like.** A simple menu lists every case and remembers the ones you have visited.
- **Read aloud.** One button steps the text aside, gives the diorama the whole window, and reads the page to you.
- **All of it drawn by code.** Every scene, model, and sound is made as you go. No photographs, no downloaded models, no recorded music.

## The text

The whole book is also here as one plain page: [THE GATELESS GATE](THE-GATELESS-GATE.md).
It is generated from the same source the interactive edition reads, so the two cannot drift apart.

The English is Nyogen Senzaki and Paul Reps's rendering of the Mumonkan, privately printed by John Murray in Los Angeles in 1934.
That printing is in the United States public domain, because its copyright was never renewed.
Reps later expanded the material into *Zen Flesh, Zen Bones* (1957), the version most readers know.
A few archaic verb forms and pronouns survive in the capping verses and have been lightly modernized.
Mumon's commentaries were already in plain modern English.

The 1934 printing carries the cases and nothing else.
Mumon's preface, his afterword, the Zen Warnings and Amban's letter were translated for this edition from the Chinese of the Taishō canon (CBETA T48n2005).
There were three independent passes, each made blind to the others and to any existing English version, then compared line by line, with every difference in meaning settled against the Chinese.
It is a new rendering and worth reading as one: it has had no scholarly review.
A few other small editorial changes were made along the way, and the book's own About page has the details.

The goal was something beautiful enough to draw in someone who has never heard of a koan, and faithful enough to reward someone who has.

## Still to come

This is a living project. Some of what's planned:

- **Richer scenes.** Better composition and more variety as the kit of parts grows, and more of each koan's particular weather.
- **A better reading.** Every case is read by a synthesized voice today. The whole book can be re-baked automatically, so the reading improves as the technology does.
- **More languages.** The text and its reading in other tongues, and one day the original Chinese beside the English, read aloud in its own voice.
- **Supplemental material.** Who these old masters were, some history, a pronunciation guide, and where to read further. An index of the people the book keeps returning to, each one leading back into the cases they walk through.
- **Offline.** Installable, so the whole book travels with you.
- **A koan a day.** One case each morning, if you'd like one.
